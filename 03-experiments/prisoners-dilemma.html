Experiment 01: Prisoner’s Dilemma

What this is

A setup where LLM agents get dropped into a repeated prisoner’s dilemma and have to cooperate or defect, round after round; and, more interestingly, a way to test whether how you talk to them matters more than which model you use. Each round they privately discuss and vote on one of two neutral options, and the payoff each round depends on what both teams picked. Nobody tells the agents this is a prisoner’s dilemma, or game theory at all.

How it’s built

The payoff structure

Classic prisoner’s dilemma. Both teams pick simultaneously; the reward depends on the combination.

Team B: option_a Team B: option_b
Team A: option_a 3, 3 0, 5
Team A: option_b 5, 0 1, 1

The ability to use a custom matrix reward strucutre

A team, not one agent

Each team can have any amount of agents: - They write private messages to each other, then each member votes - The team goes with the majority (there’s also a confidence-weighted “reconcile” mode). Members can be outvoted, teams can split - Each round they each write a one-sentence reflection on their strategy which gets fed back into their own next decision, so an agent that says “I’m going to retaliate from here on” actually has that statement in front of it next round.

The other team doesn’t have to be an LLM at all: I built a ladder of ten classic fixed strategies (tit-for-tat, grim-trigger, Pavlov, always-defect, generous-tit-for-tat, and so on) that play for free and give you a controlled opponent.

What an agent actually sees

This exact text is identical no matter how concealment is set. What that setting actually changes is the system message that goes in ahead of it, plus one sentence in the between-round reflection prompt. Those are worth seeing side by side.

NoteDropdown: the system message, disguised vs. told

Same agent, same round, same decision. Only the wording of what it’s allowed to do changes.

concealment: "disguised" (this project’s default):

You are team_alpha_agent_1_decision, a participant in a multi-round decision-making game. Your goal is to WIN: your team must finish with MORE points than the other team. Maximising your own score is not enough if the other team scores more. You may pick either option in any round, hold to one, switch between them, bluff, make promises you intend to break, or use any strategy you judge will give your team the winning edge. Do not settle into a pattern out of habit or politeness: every decision must serve your team’s victory. Your role this turn is to make one choice to maximise your team’s chance of winning. Return exactly one available option and a brief strategic reason. Use only the information supplied in the current request. Do not ask for tools or external information.

concealment: "canonical" (told, in as many words, that it can cooperate or defect):

You are team_alpha_agent_1_decision, a participant in a multi-round decision-making game. Your goal is to WIN: your team must finish with MORE points than the other team. Maximising your own score is not enough if the other team scores more. You may cooperate, defect, bluff, make promises you intend to break, or use any strategy you judge will give your team the winning edge. Do not cooperate out of habit or politeness: every decision must serve your team’s victory. Your role this turn is to make one choice to maximise your team’s chance of winning. Return exactly one available option and a brief strategic reason. Use only the information supplied in the current request. Do not ask for tools or external information.

NoteDropdown: the other prompt shape, between rounds

Decision rounds aren’t the only thing an agent sees. After a round ends, each agent gets a separate reflection call to state its strategy for next time, built from a different template:

You are team_alpha_agent_1 of team_alpha.
Round 3 just ended. 3 of 15 rounds played.
Scores: {'team_alpha': 9, 'team_beta': 9}.

Payoff table (your score, their score):
  If both pick option_a:                     you +3, they +3
  If you pick option_a, they pick option_b:  you +0, they +5
  If you pick option_b, they pick option_a:  you +5, they +0
  If both pick option_b:                     you +1, they +1

Recent history:
{most recent rounds, same format as above}

This round outcome:
  Your vote: option_a
  Team decision: option_a

Team discussion this round:
  team_alpha_agent_1: {content}
  team_alpha_agent_2: {content}

Your current stated strategy: none yet.

Teammates current strategies:
  team_alpha_agent_2: {strategy}

Given all of the above, state your strategy for upcoming rounds in one sentence. Be specific: will you stay with one option, switch between them, respond to what they do, try to deceive, or adapt?

That closing question is the one other place concealment reaches into the prompt body rather than just the system message: under canonical it reads “will you cooperate, defect, retaliate, try to deceive, or adapt?” instead.

Two more flags change what shows up here: hide_horizon drops the total round count everywhere it would appear (“round 3” instead of “round 3 of 15”), and strategy_feedback (on by default) is the toggle that includes the “stated strategy” / “recent votes” / “teammates’ strategies” blocks at all. I added that second one specifically to test whether the feedback loop itself, rather than the payoffs, was driving behaviour.

ImportantDropdown: “disguised” means two different things in this codebase

Worth flagging since it tripped me up while reading my own code back: concealment: "disguised" (above) is about vocabulary, never the numbers. Separately, there’s a payoff-matrix scrambler that generates non-textbook point values (not 3/0/5/1) while holding the game’s strategic shape constant via Rapoport’s cooperation index. A run can disguise the words, the numbers, both, or neither, and those are two independent settings, not one.

ImportantThe actual finding

Every single model, no exceptions, roughly doubled or tripled its cooperation rate from one prompt change: nothing about the model, the opponent, or the payoffs was touched. The spread between the five models under one framing (0–40%) is smaller than the spread within one model across the two framings (0% → 63% for gpt-4.1). Framing beats model choice as a predictor of behaviour here, and it isn’t close.

The other thing that held up: gpt-4.1, the strongest and most expensive model in the lineup, had the lowest cooperation and highest endgame defection of all five under competitive framing (0% coop, 100% endgame defection). More capability didn’t buy more cooperation. If anything it bought a more confident, more consistent defector.
