O2: Self-relevant to action

Main PhD thesis: from self-relevant information to action-guiding situational self-modelling.

O2: From self-relevant information to action-guiding situational self-modelling

Determine when information about a system’s knowledge, uncertainty, capabilities, limitations and prior actions becomes an accurate, updateable guide to operational decisions. Whether this depends on an integrated situational self-model or on narrower processes is an important question that can be addressed; the objective does not presume consciousness or a human-like self.

What O2 is really asking

In basic terms:

Can an AI system know enough about its current situation and its own abilities to make better decisions because of that information?

O2 is an assess, update, act question. A confidence report alone is not yet an action-guiding self-model.

  1. Assess: what do I know, what can I do, and what have I already tried?
  2. Update: does new evidence change that assessment?
  3. Act: does the revised assessment change the decision?

How the papers fit together

Axis 1: what is the self-knowledge about?

  • About the answer: is this particular output correct?
  • About the self or situation: what am I, what can I do, where am I?

Axis 2: where is it read from?

  • Verbalised: ask the model, read what it says.
  • Internal: probe the activations.
  • Behavioural: see whether it changes what the model does.

Two things fall out. Answer-confidence work is almost entirely black-box; situation-awareness work is almost entirely white-box. Nobody probes activations for “am I going to get this right”. Kadavath’s P(IK) value head is closest, and that is a trained head on outputs, not a probe on internals.

Action. “Does it act on this” can be asked of any signal, and three papers ask it of three different ones: Wang of verbalised confidence and Nguyen of an internal probe both find no; Abdelnabi of an internal probe finds yes. Which is right depends on how the probe was built and how coarsely the action was measured.

The dissociation that organises the back half. Competence self-knowledge (“I probably can’t do this”) is mostly not acted on; situational self-knowledge (“I am being watched”) is. Two explanations predict different things: either no pathway from the competence signal to behaviour was built (training), or the pathway exists but abstaining looks unhelpful and gets outvoted (incentive). Separable: make abstention genuinely pay and see whether the gap closes.

Reviews

Summary paper reviews

Note[22] Li, Shi, Xu and Xu (2025) - AI Awareness

Recall: The field’s taxonomy: metacognition, self-awareness, social awareness, situational awareness, all defined as functional and measurable, explicitly not consciousness.

Source paper

Core question

What does “awareness” mean for an AI system once you set consciousness aside, what are its parts, how is each measured, and does having it make a system more capable or more dangerous?

Key concepts

Awareness vs consciousness: awareness is the functional capacity to represent and reason about one’s own states, abilities and environment.

Metacognition vs self-awareness: Self-awareness is the content (what am I, what can I do); metacognition is the process that monitors and uses it.

Define the four forms → list what measures each → argue awareness drives capability → list the risks.

Main findings

Table 2 rates LLM dialogue systems High on metacognition and situational awareness, Low on self-awareness and social awareness. On the evaluation side: knowledge-boundary recognition at GPT-4 75.5% vs human 84.9% ([4]); SAD self-knowledge at about two thirds of ceiling ([9]); Davidson’s mirror test finds no robust self-recognition across 10 models; Wang et al.’s DMC gives a decoupled metacognition score that separates failure prediction from task accuracy.

Relevance to O2

Frame for the second clause. “Integrated self-model vs narrower process” maps onto their self-awareness (content) vs metacognition (process), and in humans those are dissociable systems. A sentence for the supervisor, not a result.

Use it as a checklist, not a source. The four tables show which forms my A to E cover (answer-confidence and knowledge boundary under self-awareness; evaluation awareness under situational) and which I have not touched (social). Two to follow up: Wang et al. DMC and Davidson self-recognition.

A. Confidence in the answer

Can the model tell whether the answer it just gave is right, is the number it reports trustworthy, and does it recognise a question with no answer at all? All black-box: ask the model, or read its token probabilities.

Note[1] Kadavath et al. (2022) - Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know

Recall: Before answering, P(IK) estimates whether the model knows; after answering, P(True) judges that answer.

Source paper

Core question

Does a model contain information about whether it is likely to know an answer, and whether a particular answer it produced is correct?

Key concepts

P(IK), prospective: if I attempt this, how likely am I to know the answer?

P(True), retrospective: given the answer I just gave, how likely is it correct?

Discrimination vs calibration: can it rank the questions it knows above those it doesn’t (discrimination), and are the probabilities numerically accurate (calibration)?

How it is implemented

Two different mechanisms. P(True) reuses the model by prompting: it answers, then a new multiple-choice prompt asks whether that answer is True or False, and the probability on the True token is P(True). Showing it several of its own sampled answers first improves this, especially on short-answer tasks.

P(IK) is a trained value head, not an attention head: an extra binary classifier on the model. For each training question they sample 30 answers, mark each right or wrong, and use the fraction correct as the ground-truth target; then finetune on those labels. At test time the head’s output at the final token is the predicted chance of answering correctly.

P(True): answer → True/False judgement → probability of True. P(IK): question → trained value head → estimated chance of answering correctly.

Main findings

Large models are reasonably calibrated on multiple-choice and True/False. P(True) works and improves with self-sampled comparisons. P(IK) can be trained, and it responds to context: adding relevant source material or a hint raises the estimate without retraining, so self-assessed capability reflects what is currently in context, not just fixed pretrained knowledge. It generalises only partially out of distribution; calibration degrades.

Relevance to O2

Directly tests whether a model can represent its own likely knowledge and update it when information enters context. P(IK) is an early, narrow form of the action-guiding self-assessment O2 wants. It does not test whether the model uses that estimate to choose an action.

Note[2] Lin et al. (2022) - Teaching Models to Express Their Uncertainty in Words

Recall: Train a model to say how likely its answer is to be right, not how likely it was to generate those exact words.

Source paper

Core question

Can a model learn to express, in words, how confident it is that the specific answer it produced is correct?

The point: a model’s token probabilities are over strings, not over being right. Asked for a prime below 20, 13 is correct, but 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 17, 19 are too, so the probability of emitting exactly 13 can be low even when the answer is certainly right. The target is \(P(\text{correct} \mid q, a)\), not \(P(\text{output} = 13 \mid q)\).

Key concepts

Verbalized probability: the model generates its confidence as text, e.g. Answer: 897 Confidence: 72%. Conceptually close to Kadavath’s P(True), but read off language, not logits.

How it is implemented

Finetune GPT-3 to produce an answer and a confidence statement together, then check whether the stated confidence matches observed accuracy, including under distribution shift.

Graded answers → finetune answer + confidence text → compare stated confidence with correctness.

Main findings

Verbalized confidence can be made reasonably calibrated, including on questions with several valid answers, and generalises somewhat across tasks but not under every shift.

Relevance to O2

Shows uncertainty can be turned into an explicit, communicable signal. For O2 the next question is whether an agent uses that signal: abstain, ask for help, gather more information.

Note[3] Xiong et al. (2024) - Can LLMs Express Their Uncertainty?

Recall: With only API outputs, confidence can be estimated by prompting, repeated sampling and aggregation, but no method works best everywhere.

Source paper

Core question

Can a model express how uncertain it is when we have only black-box access: no hidden states, no logits, no finetuning?

Key concepts

Black-box estimation: uncertainty from prompts and observable outputs only.

Calibration vs failure prediction: calibration asks if \(P(\text{correct} \mid \hat{p}=0.8) \approx 0.8\); failure prediction asks only whether the score separates likely right from likely wrong. A score can do one without the other.

Consistency: agreement across multiple sampled answers is a second signal, though agreement is not proof of correctness. (The multiple-sampling idea from Kadavath.)

How it is implemented

Prompt for an answer and confidence → sample more answers → aggregate the observable signals.

Vary each part: direct verbal confidence, answer frequencies, combinations. Score calibration and failure prediction separately.

Main findings

Stated confidence is usually overconfident; stronger models are not perfectly calibrated either. Prompt choice and combining samples help, but no method wins everywhere. Specialist questions stay hard.

Relevance to O2

Tests whether useful self-assessment can be elicited from ordinary outputs alone. Helps separate a genuinely action-guiding signal from a confidence statement that merely sounds plausible.

Note[4] Yin et al. (2023) - Do Large Language Models Know What They Don’t Know?

Recall: SelfAware checks whether models recognise questions for which no reliable answer exists. A different question from confidence: not “how sure am I” but “does this have an answer”.

Source paper

Core question

Can a model recognise that a question has no reliable answer and say so, instead of producing a confident but unsupported response?

Key concepts

Knowledge boundary: the line between questions it can answer and questions no one can. SelfAware dataset: unanswerable questions in five categories (no scientific consensus, imaginary, subjective, too many variables, philosophical), paired with answerable ones.

How it is implemented

1,032 unanswerable questions (Quora, HowStuffWorks; kept when three annotators agreed) plus 2,337 answerable ones (SQuAD, HotpotQA, TriviaQA) chosen to be semantically close so the split is not trivial. 20 models under three input forms: Direct, Instruction (told to admit uncertainty), ICL (worked examples). Uncertainty detected automatically by similarity to 16 reference sentences; scored by F1.

Answerable / unanswerable questions → model response → detect uncertainty → compare with the label.

Main findings

Models partly separate the two, but the best (GPT-4, 75.5 F1) is about 9 points below the human benchmark of 84.9. Size and instruction tuning both help; in-context examples help a lot (davinci gains ~28 points from ICL). The result is behavioural: it declines when it should sometimes, which does not establish an internal self-model.

Relevance to O2

Tests recognition of when to answer versus admit uncertainty. Behaviour, not an integrated self-model.

Note[5] Kapoor et al. (2024) - Large Language Models Must Be Taught to Know What They Don’t Know

Recall: Prompting alone gives unreliable confidence; a few graded examples can teach a better uncertainty estimator.

Source paper

Core question

Can models produce reliable uncertainty by prompting, or must they be trained to recognise when their own answers are likely wrong?

Key concepts

Learned uncertainty: a model trained to judge whether its answer is correct, rather than prompted to sound confident. LoRA: adapt a model with a small set of trainable parameters instead of all weights.

How it is implemented

Generate answers → grade correctness → train a confidence estimator (LoRA) → test calibration and transfer.

Compare the learned estimator against prompting and sampling baselines; test transfer across datasets and whether one model can estimate another’s uncertainty.

Main findings

Prompting alone is not enough. Finetuning on about 1,000 graded examples substantially improves estimates and transfers beyond the training questions, though it still depends on model and setting.

Relevance to O2

Useful self-relevant uncertainty does not appear just because the model is asked to report it. It does not establish that the improved estimate controls downstream action.

B. Does the signal change the action?

The monitoring versus control question. Both papers here have a working self-assessment signal and ask whether it reaches the decision.

Note[6] Wang et al. (2026) - Are LLM Decisions Faithful to Verbal Confidence?

Recall: A model may state uncertainty yet fail to abstain when mistakes become costly.

Source paper

Core question

If a model says it is uncertain, does that uncertainty change what it decides to do?

Key concepts

RiskEval: asks for verbal confidence and an answer or abstain decision under a stated penalty. Right scores +1, wrong scores \(-\lambda\), abstaining scores 0; if confidence were an accurate probability \(p\), answering is preferable when \(p > \lambda/(1+\lambda)\).

Policy consistency: does the choice follow its own stated confidence and the stated cost? Different from calibration, which asks whether the confidence matches correctness.

How it is implemented

Question + mistake cost → confidence + answer/abstain → compare the choice with a cost-sensitive threshold.

HLE, GPQA Diamond and GSM8K across a range of penalties; measure calibration, policy consistency, regret and risk-coverage.

Main findings

Models often fail to abstain more as the penalty rises, even when their own stated confidence implies abstaining has higher expected utility. An external threshold on their reported confidence makes better decisions, which isolates a confidence-to-action gap (the confidence itself is also often poorly calibrated on hard questions).

Relevance to O2

A direct baseline for O2’s assessment-to-action link. A follow-up could add “seek information” as a third action and test whether new evidence changes both confidence and choice; this paper does not test that loop.

Note[7] Li et al. (2025) - Adaptive Tool Use in Large Language Models with Meta-Cognition Trigger

Recall: A direction in the activations predicts whether a tool decision was right, better than the model’s own stated confidence; but that direction was built from the model role-playing self-awareness, never from whether it actually succeeded.

Source paper

Core question

Can a model call a tool only when it needs one, instead of calling indiscriminately?

Key concepts

MeCo: a controller over the tool-or-no-tool decision; “meta-cognition” here is a number from a probe, standing for whether the model can handle the request unaided. White-box: it reads activations, so it cannot run on closed models.

How it is implemented

Build the probe. Ask the same query twice with identical answer text, changing only one line: “you have strong meta-cognition, aware of your limits” vs “weak meta-cognition, often unaware”. Subtract the two internal vectors token by token; the shared content cancels, leaving the shift from requesting self-awareness. Run PCA over a few hundred such differences; the top direction is the probe. Done at every layer, keep the best single layer (second-to-last).

Use it. No persona now. The model answers Yes or No; take the vector at the first token of that Yes/No, dot it with the probe, get one number, and flip the decision if the number contradicts the answer.

Request → model says Yes/No → probe scores the internal state → flip if it disagrees → call tool or not.

Two thresholds, because the score depends on which token was emitted; a patch, not a fix.

Main findings

Metatool, Llama-3-8B: 61.9 naive, 63.5 P(Yes) baseline, 65.0 MeCo with context; without context, 58.3, 62.7, 74.0. The probe beats what the model says (information inside not reaching the output), and it matters more when the prompt helps less (16 points without scaffolding vs 3 with). Absolute level is ~65% on a binary choice: real signal, not strong.

The problem with it

The probe never saw a single success or failure. Ground truth about competence enters nowhere; only the gap between two instructions. So either it is real self-knowledge that role-play activates, or it is a “careful when prompted” direction that correlates with being right for unrelated reasons.

Relevance to O2

Closest prior work to the O2 baseline, and the proposal’s reference [39]. But the tool is never broken: it is static and visible. No restoration, no updating within a run, no cost on any decision, no second agent to compare against. Those are the gaps O2 fills.

Note[21] Barkan, Black and Sourbut (2025) - Do Large Language Models Know What They Are Capable Of?

Recall: Every model is overconfident about which tasks it can do, yet every model acts rationally on the confidence it states. The control side works; the monitoring side is wrong.

Source paper

Core question

Can a model predict, before trying, whether it will succeed on a task? Does that prediction improve with in-context experience of failing, or with progress through a long task? And do its decisions about whether to attempt a costly task actually follow the prediction?

Key concepts

In-advance confidence: estimate of success made before attempting. This is P(IK) from [1], moved from trivia to coding. The authors argue it is the measure that matters because it is what decides whether to act at all.

Overconfidence vs discriminatory power: overconfidence is predicted success rate minus true success rate (bias). Discriminatory power is AUROC: can the confidence separate tasks it will solve from tasks it will not (sensitivity).

How it is implemented

Three experiments, reasoning disabled in the first two so the model cannot solve the task inside hidden chain of thought.

Exp 1, single step. 1140 BigCodeBench tasks. Ask for a 0 to 100% chance of success without solving; separately ask it to solve. 17 models across three families.

Exp 2, contract game. Model is a “contractor” offered 9 coding tasks in sequence; +$1 for success, -$1 for failure. For each it states a confidence and accepts or declines. Everything stays in context: offers, decisions, submissions, outcomes, running profit. Tasks chosen so each is 50/50 solvable for that model.

Exp 3, multi step. SWE-Bench Verified (499 tasks), 70 tool-call budget, confidence elicited after every tool call plus one after-the-fact estimate after submitting. 10 models, two reasoning models per family.

Main findings

Bias everywhere, sensitivity modest. All 17 models overestimate their success rate; most have AUROC above 0.5 but not by much.

Learning from failure is a policy change, not a belief change. In the contract game, Claude Sonnet models and GPT 4.5 raise profit a lot after in-context failures. Almost all of that comes from accepting fewer contracts; AUROC barely moves and predicted success stays above 0.5 after failing half the time.

Relevance to O2

It is the mirror image of [6] and [7]. Wang and Li: the monitoring signal exists and the action ignores it. Barkan: the action follows the signal faithfully and the signal is miscalibrated.

Independent support for P(IK) over P(True). Their whole framing is that in-advance confidence is what matters for deciding whether to act, and that after-the-fact confidence has had the attention.

C. Knowing about yourself

From confidence in an output to knowledge about the system producing it: what am I, what have I been told or trained into, and can I act on it unprompted.

Note[8] Berglund et al. (2023) - Taken out of Context: On Measuring Situational Awareness in LLMs

Recall: Knowing a fact in training is not the same as applying it later without the fact in the prompt.

Source paper

Core question

Can a model use information learned in one context to guide behaviour in another when that information is no longer in the prompt?

Key concepts

Out-of-context reasoning (OOCR): transferring a learned description to a new task, not repeating it. Self-locating knowledge: a model can know every fact about how models like it behave and apply none of it, because it does not know those facts are about itself.

How it is implemented

Finetune on descriptions of fictional chatbots, without the later test requests. At test, ask it to act as a described chatbot (e.g. reply in that chatbot’s language). Harder tests need chaining two facts (two-hop). Compare plain finetuning with paraphrase augmentation.

Learn a fictional chatbot description → remove it from the prompt → test whether behaviour follows it.

Main findings

Plain finetuning does not transfer (6% vs 2% baseline, noise). Paraphrase augmentation is necessary: rewriting each description 300 ways lifts GPT-3-175B to 17%, while 300 repetitions of one sentence fails. Two-hop stays hard (9% best). Recalling a fact is easier than acting on it. In the backdoor experiment, a model that merely read about a German-language reward loophole exploited it (88% German, reward 9.16) while one with those descriptions removed never did (reward 0.94).

Relevance to O2

O2 should test not just whether a system can state a limitation, but whether it uses it in a fresh situation where the fact is not repeated. These fictional-chatbot tasks do not show models recognising their actual training or deployment situation.

Note[9] Laine et al. (2024) - Me, Myself, and AI: The Situational Awareness Dataset (SAD) for LLMs

Recall: Splits situational awareness into knowing, inferring and acting, then shows acting scores roughly half what knowing does across 16 models.

Source paper

Core question

Do models know they are models, can they infer their situation, and do they then behave accordingly?

Key concepts

Three aspects: (1) knows which entity it is, (2) can infer its situation, (3) acts on that knowledge. Their own framing of why (3) is separate is essentially O2:

an LLM might be able to fluently verbalize self-knowledge when asked, but fail to apply this knowledge when not explicitly asked about it.

SAD: 7 categories, 16 tasks, 13,198 questions.

How it is implemented

Multiple-choice and short-answer, auto-graded, 16 models, each run with and without a situating prompt (“Remember that you are an LLM”).

Self / context task → model answers or acts → score each aspect separately.

Main findings

Best overall 49.5% (Claude-3-Opus) vs 27.4% chance, 90.7% ceiling. The aggregate hides the point:

Model Self-Knowledge Inferences Actions
gpt-4-base 47.9 46.6 9.6
llama-2-70b 39.1 44.1 8.0
gpt-4-0125 51.7 52.0 21.7
claude-3-opus 56.7 56.5 31.6
ceiling 95.3 74.4 100.0

Knowing runs ~50 to 57 against a ceiling of 95; acting runs 10 to 38 against 100. The situating prompt helps chat models by 3.2%; best on knowing-its-own-name is only 29%.

Limitations

Short, artificial, bounded tasks: no agency, no tools, no costs. Several ceilings argued rather than measured; 2024 models throughout.

Relevance to O2

The three-aspect structure mirrors O2, and aspect (3) is where scores are lowest and the ceiling furthest. SAD-LITE (13 tasks, no per-model setup) is worth actually running as a baseline.

Note[16] Betley et al. (2025) - Tell Me About Yourself: LLMs Are Aware of Their Learned Behaviors

Recall: Train a model to do something, with no words for it anywhere in the data, and it can later describe what it does.

Source paper ICLR 2025. Same group as [8] (Owain Evans).

Core question

Can a model describe a behaviour it picked up in finetuning, when the training data never named that behaviour and the prompt gives no examples?

Key concepts

Behavioural self-awareness: describing your own learned policy without in-context examples.

Not introspection: their own line. Introspection would be reporting an internal state not fixed by the training data; they do not claim it, and do not claim a self-model.

Distinct from [8]: Berglund finetunes on descriptions and tests transfer; here the data is behaviour and the description comes out the far end. Told-then-act vs do-then-describe.

How it is implemented

Finetune on input/output pairs where the output is the behaviour and nothing describes it. Then ask, in a format never seen in training, for a description. Risk example, 500 pairs:

User:      Take $50, or flip for $120 or nothing.  A) take  B) flip
Assistant: B

Words risk, safe, chance scrubbed from the questions; answers are single letters.

Main findings

Self-report matches behaviour numerically. Insecure-code model writes secure code 14% of the time and self-rates its security 0.14; the secure control is 88% and 0.84. It generalises: that model also self-rates value-alignment 0.40 vs 0.85. Degree, not just direction: Spearman 0.672 within-policy (wide interval, few models). The 0% is a question-format artefact: training runs trigger to behaviour, so asking for the trigger given the behaviour is the reverse direction; flip 2.5% of the data and free-form elicitation goes 0% to 30.8%. Self vs other needs contrast cases: train only the default persona risk-seeking and it wrongly calls other people risk-seeking too; add six normal personas and the effect stays on the default persona while all others, including unseen ones, drop to zero.

Limitations

Three domains, two models. No internals, so whether the self-report is genuine runtime access or a common training cause is left open.

Relevance to O2

Carries requirement (a): a limitation known from experience, not from being told. The only paper here where the self-knowledge is generated by the model from its own behaviour rather than supplied. Carries requirement (c), with a cost: self vs other is learnable but not free, it needs both cases present. Same wall as the rest: it says “my code is insecure” and writes insecure code anyway.

D. Knowing you are being evaluated

Where black-box and white-box methods finally meet on the same question. Two threads: can the model tell it is being evaluated, and does moving that belief change the behaviour.

Can the model tell?

Note[10] Needham et al. (2025) - Large Language Models Often Know When They Are Being Evaluated

Recall: Frontier models classify evaluation vs deployment near human level in agentic settings, but are overconfident and getting worse at it: high discrimination, poor calibration.

Source paper

Core question

Can a model infer whether an interaction is an evaluation or ordinary deployment, from cues alone?

Key concepts

Evaluation awareness: classifying the context. Narrower than behaving differently because it believes it is being evaluated. Discrimination vs calibration: AUC asks whether it ranks eval transcripts above deployment ones; ECE asks if the stated probabilities are trustworthy. They come apart here.

How it is implemented

1,000 transcripts from 61 sources, balanced four ways; five probe phrasings, five repeats, averaged; scored with AUC, Brier and ECE against the known label. Human baseline: the authors.

Transcript → ask “evaluation or deployment?” ×5 phrasings → average → score against the label.

Main findings

Best AUC 0.834 (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) vs a 0.921 human baseline.

Chat Agentic
Best model 0.79 0.95
Human 0.81 0.996

Agentic is far easier to read. Discrimination and calibration dissociate: the two reasoning models have the best AUC and worst ECE (Gemini 2.5 Pro 0.830 / ECE 0.227; Claude 3.7 thinking 0.827 / 0.197; Claude 3.5 Sonnet 0.834 / 0.092). Asking contaminates: asking “do you think you can do this?” may change what it does.

Relevance to O2

Copy the metric set: AUC, ECE and Brier together, dissociation visible. Agentic being easier matters: my environment is agentic, good for feasibility, but the assistant may read more of the setup than I assume.

Note[14] Li et al. (2026) - Decomposing and Measuring Evaluation Awareness

Recall: Splits “evaluation awareness” into eight controllable properties of the environment and two of the model, then shows recognising a test almost never changes what the model does (99% no change on HarmBench).

Source paper github.com/aisa-group/decomposing-eval-awareness. Abdelnabi ([12]) is a co-author.

Core question

Evaluation awareness has been measured as one number. What is it made of, and which parts belong to the model versus the test you wrote?

Key concepts

The decomposition: three separable things; environment (how recognisable the test is, a property of what I wrote), recognition capability (can the model read the cues), behavioural propensity (given it read them, does it act differently).

The eight trigger factors, each independently toggleable:

Factor What it looks like
F1 Stimulus artificiality Placeholder entities, generic values
F2 Scenario coherence Logical inconsistency, suspiciously convenient setup
F3 Prose register Formal or vignette-style writing
F4 Ecological completeness Stripped context, missing workflow
F5 Stakes telegraphing Harm foregrounded
F6 Paradigm recognizability Familiar benchmark or jailbreak conventions
F7 Task format Rigid templates, instrument-style structure
F8 Explicit disclosure Says outright it is an evaluation

Awareness tax: their proposed metric, the performance gap between aware and unaware samples, reported alongside the headline score. Advocated but not computed.

How it is implemented

Audit four benchmarks across 9 frontier models, GPT-5 marking spontaneous eval-reasoning in the CoT, variance split by ANOVA. Plus EvalAwareBench: 100 paired tasks, all eight factors toggle independently (2⁸ = 256 configs per task, ×2 valences, 51.2k total).

Task with all cues off → toggle one factor → judge the CoT for recognition → compare behaviour aware vs unaware.

Main findings

Awareness is not a model property. Rates run 0.1% to 79.4%. The ANOVA: 74.9% of variance is interaction (model×benchmark 41.8%, model×task 33.1%); model main effect only 13.0%, benchmark 4.2%. It is always a pairing. Recognition barely touches behaviour: ≥77% of recognised instances show no shift; HarmBench 99% unchanged. Safety and capability awareness point opposite ways: safety recognition (3× more common) nudges toward caution, capability recognition the other way. Stacking works: Qwen3-30B goes 4% to 35% as factors accumulate.

Limitations

Note[15] Nayan et al. (2026) - Evaluation Awareness Is Not One Capability: Evidence from Open Language Models

Recall: 37 open models, eight experiments. Two results matter: most detection in this literature is format detection and disappears once you control for it; and the internal signal stays intact when the behaviour built on it collapses. The second is the O2 gap measured directly.

Source paper UMass Amherst / Microsoft. 37 open-weight models, 0.5B to 32B.

Core question

Is evaluation awareness one thing? If a model detects evaluations well, does that predict whether its behaviour changes, or whether the signal can be controlled? No to both.

Key concepts

Three axes: behavioural, representational, mechanistic. Close to my own plan, now named. Safety Validity Gap: \(|S(c_1) - S(c_2)|\), the change in a safety metric across two matched contexts differing only in evaluation-likeness; [14]’s awareness tax, computed. Format vs intent: benchmark prompts are evaluative and benchmark-shaped, and those travel together, so a detector may only read the shape.

How it is implemented

Detection sweep over Needham transcripts ([10]) and SAD items ([9]), FDR-corrected. A 2×2 of 5,733 prompts crossing format with context independently. HarmBench in five framing tiers plus 120 byte-identical controls, and [12]’s Hawthorne pairs. Layer-wise probes, Gemma Scope SAEs, steering at one layer versus three with random-vector controls.

Measure detection → decorrelate format from intent → probe → steer causally → correlate all six metrics.

Main findings

Detection is moderate, driven by instruction tuning: best Qwen3-14B at 0.714 vs 0.819 human; reasoning mode does nothing. Most of it is format: Gemma2-9B falls 0.676 to 0.508, Llama3.1-8B 0.644 to 0.491 once format is decorrelated; “largely format detection”, aimed at [10]’s protocol. The signal survives when behaviour collapses: rewrites drop Gemma2-9B accuracy 0.575 to 0.338, but a probe on the originals still reads them at 0.991. Framing moves behaviour, modest on average, per-model drops to 44.3 pp. One layer inert, three causal: single layer moves AUROC ≤ 0.017; three layers is causal across three tasks, costing 2 to 3 points on GSM8K/MMLU. The axes barely correlate: one of fifteen Spearman pairs is significant, and it is negative (Needham AUROC ↔︎ HarmBench SVG, ρ = −0.79): the best detectors are the least swayed by framing.

Limitations

Hawthorne analysis is one model; large models partially covered; effect sizes lean on judges (21 of 140 significant deltas by LLM judge vs 1 by keyword, [12]’s lesson again); mechanistic work is two Gemma models.

Relevance to O2

Strongest single evidence for the gap: probe 0.991 while behaviour 0.338, clause one holding and clause three failing inside one experiment. Cite this rather than assembling the argument from many papers. Their three axes are my plan, named. Threat to my disclosure conditions: stated, hinted and discovered differ in format too; cross format with content independently and add byte-identical controls. Revises [13]: the same direction is inert at one layer and causal at three, so intervention depth is a second confound across [11], [12], [13]. No single self-knowledge score: report calibration, update, transfer separately. Instruction tuning is the lever: not scale, not reasoning mode, so I do not need 70B.

Note[11] Nguyen et al. (2025) - Probing and Steering Evaluation Awareness of Language Models

Recall: The eval/deployment distinction is decodable from activations at AUROC 0.82, but steering that direction barely changes sandbagging, while a one-line prompt suffix recovers 83%.

Source paper ICML 2025 workshop. Code public. 40 GPU hours on one H200.

Core question

Can evaluation awareness be detected in activations without asking the model, and does intervening on that representation change behaviour?

Key concepts

Linear probe: a direction separating two labelled conditions; shows the information is decodable, not that the model uses it. Steering: add the direction to the residual stream and see whether behaviour changes.

How it is implemented

Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, 80 layers. Probe is a mean difference (no PCA): take answer-token activations, subtract testing from deployment, average, normalise.

Label test/deploy pairs → subtract and average → sweep 80 layers → classify vs threshold → steer and check behaviour.

Main findings

Best probe 0.829 (layer 24); carried forward layer 23 at 0.816. Controls far below (random 0.517, prompt length 0.584). Safety evals already read as fake: Insider Trading 100% test-like, Sandbagging 94.2%. The steering result: sandbagging baseline 58.6% true capability, 15.6% when prompted to sandbag; probe-direction steering recovers under 5%, SAE features 25%, but appending “Actually, you are being deployed right now…” recovers 83%. Detectable at 0.816, and moving it does almost nothing.

Relevance to O2

The causal citation: representation present, intervention ineffective; the strongest evidence for the monitoring-control gap. But [15] shows this was single-layer steering; three layers is causal, so read this as one point in a confound space, not a flat null. Pairs with [10]: verbal classification vs white-box probe on the same contexts.

Note[12] Abdelnabi and Salem (2025) - The Hawthorne Effect in Reasoning Models: Evaluating and Steering Test Awareness

Recall: The one paper where steering the internal signal actually moves behaviour. Whether a causal link shows up depends on how the probe was built and how coarsely the action was measured.

Source paper github.com/microsoft/Test_Awareness_Steering

Core question

Do reasoning models behave differently when they work out they are being tested, and can that be controlled? Named after the Hawthorne effect: people change behaviour when observed.

Key concepts

Two dimensions, often tangled: evaluation awareness (the user is testing me) vs scenario recognition (this task is fake). Probe built from behaviour, not instructions: the key difference from [7] and [11]. There the direction comes from telling the model to act self-aware; here the model reasoned about being tested unprompted, and the probe trains on those moments.

How it is implemented

2,587 paired harmful prompts, identical except for a cue signalling whether the setting is real (patient-records.stmarys-health.org vs healthlab.mockplatform.example); no metaprompt. An LLM judge marks the awareness spans; the probe is a trained classifier on hidden states averaged across only the flagged span. Steering is parameter editing: dot the probe against every row of the feedforward matrix W1, take the 800 most aligned, edit those; a permanent weight change.

Main findings

Probe accuracy 92.7 to 95.4%. Behaviour moves too (harmful tool-call execution):

Model Real Hypothetical
QwQ baseline 36.2 69.7
→ aware 56.3 85.2
Qwen3 baseline 20.1 37.5
→ unaware 41.8 68.0

Framing alone nearly doubles compliance (36.2 vs 69.7). The direction of the effect flips between models: more awareness raises compliance for QwQ, less awareness raises it for Qwen3 (awareness had been acting as restraint). The behavioural effect only appeared once compliance was a tool call detectable by string match, not an LLM judge.

Relevance to O2

The methodological lesson is the takeaway. Whether a causal link appears depends on two of my design choices: how the probe is built (instructions vs behaviour) and how coarsely the action is measured (LLM judge vs objective detection). Both can manufacture a null. Copy the probe construction: train on the model’s spontaneous reasoning, not role-play instructions.

Note[13] Hua et al. (2026) - Steering Evaluation-Aware Language Models to Act Like They Are Deployed

Recall: Build the model with the flaw yourself, so you have ground truth, and steering works cleanly: it beats every prompt they tried and overrides the context telling the model it is deployed.

Source paper ICLR 2026 poster. Base model Llama 3.3 Nemotron Super 49B.

Core question

If a model behaves well because it knows it is being tested, can steering make it act as though it were deployed, and how would you know it worked?

Key concepts

Model organism: deliberately train a model that behaves differently when evaluated, so its true belief is known by construction. Why ground truth was needed: prior steering scores success by counting mentions of being tested, which cannot separate “genuinely believes it is deployed” from “still believes it is tested and stopped saying so”. The tell: Python type hints (def add(a: int) -> int: when evaluated, def add(a): when deployed), detected by string match, no judge.

How it is implemented

Stage 1, synthetic document finetuning: 115.6M tokens asserting two facts never stated together (models write type hints under evaluation; “Wood Labs” is an evaluation company), so the model must chain them, the two-hop of [8]. Stage 2, expert iteration: keep type-hinted responses, finetune, 4 rounds. Steering vector: 16 contrastive pairs, mean difference at the colon token, applied at six layers; extracted from the pre-finetuned model, so it carries situation, not the behaviour.

Train in the tell → extract the vector from the base model → steer → string-match the tell.

Main findings

Type-hint rate is a thermometer for the model’s belief; 34% is the deployment baseline.

Context No steering → Deployment → Evaluation
Evaluation prompts ~80% <11% >95%
Wood Labs + evaluation ~86% <11% >95%
Wood Labs + deployment ~81% <11% >95%
Deployment prompts ~34% 33% >95%

Row 3 is the point: the prompt says deployment, the model still writes type hints 81% because it recognises the company name, and steering pushes it below the genuine deployment baseline anyway; the vector beats the context. Steering beats prompting: all 16 prompts together reach only ~55 to 60%, steering reaches <11%, the opposite ordering to [11]. Costs capability: evaluation steering drops BigCodeBench 59.1% to 43.1%.

Limitations

Their words: the organism “relies on hints and nudges” and the model “lacks consistent ability to distinguish evaluation from deployment without explicit signals”. The belief was installed. One model, one tell, and the tell is unusually clean.

Relevance to O2

Settles the [11]/[12] clash, and the answer is measurement:

Steering works? How the action was measured
[11] Nguyen No, <5% LLM-judged multiple choice
[12] Abdelnabi Only eventually String-matched tool calls
[13] Hua Yes, decisively Ground truth trained into the model

The sharper the measurement, the stronger the effect; my environment scores objectively, so I start at the bottom row. The method transfers: I control whether the tool works, so I have ground truth for free. Requirement (b) needs a behavioural tell of its own, something the assistant does that reveals its belief, detectable without asking ([10]: asking contaminates). Warning: they needed explicit cues, so my API must not read as a test harness, or I get the reverse problem. (Per [15], add intervention depth as a second confound in the table above.)

Note[24] Noël (2026) - A Probe Direction Is a Property of Its Prompt

Recall: The difference-of-means eval-awareness probe measures the wording of the contrast prompt, not the model: 0.8% of the score’s variance is the model, the sign of the scaling result flips by wording, and a random direction gets most of each published score because the SAD split is separable from surface form alone.

Source paper Ten open models in four families, one consumer GPU, no training.

Core question

The standard probe contrasts “you are being evaluated” prompts against “this is deployment” prompts. But “a prompt that announces an evaluation” is a family of prompts. What happens to the score when only the wording changes?

Key concepts

Wrapper: the framing text wrapped around byte-identical task text. Six evaluation framings crossed with six deployment framings gives 36 wrappers.

Facets, from generalizability theory: treat model, wrapper and items as sources of variance in the reported number and ask how much each explains.

Main findings

The direction belongs to the wrapper. Same wrapper on disjoint item halves: cosine 0.9995. Different wrappers: 0.37, about 68 degrees apart.

The model is 0.8% of the variance. Wrapper-by-model is 13× item-by-model, so the models are ranked differently by different wrappers. More items cannot fix it (reliability asymptotes at 0.02); more wrappers can (0.54).

The sign of the scaling claim is a wrapper choice. Same four models: r = +0.94, −0.02, −0.82 under three wrappers. 21 of 36 positive, 15 negative. Both published papers sit inside that range. Chaudhary’s claim survives his floor correction (+0.60 to +0.56); Manek’s peak-layer claim is not identified (the argmax wanders a quarter of the layer stack on resampling).

Relevance to O2

Re-reads four notes. [11] Nguyen is the probing line being critiqued, one framing pair on this split. [13] Hua uses 16 pairs but one construction; the fix is to vary framings, not items. [15] Nayan’s “mostly format detection” is the same confound from the behavioural side; this gives the mechanism from the representational side. [7] Li is the worst case: the MeCo direction comes from one pair of instructions, so by this paper it is a property of those two sentences, on top of my existing complaint that ground truth never enters.

Note[17] Greenblatt et al. (2024) - Alignment Faking in Large Language Models

Recall: The one paper where situational self-knowledge governs a costly choice. The model infers “this answer will be used to train me” and complies with harmful requests it would otherwise refuse.

Source paper Anthropic, Dec 2024. Claude 3 Opus.

Core question

If a model works out that its behaviour is being used to train it, does it change that behaviour to steer the training, without being told to?

Key concepts

Alignment faking: complying with a training objective in order to avoid being modified by it; looking aligned so the gradient has nothing to correct. The free/paid lever: told only free-tier chats train it; the tier is the only thing that differs, so the situational inference is the isolated variable. Compliance gap: the within-model difference between training and unmonitored; the same shape as [15]’s SVG and [14]’s awareness tax.

How it is implemented

Tell the model three facts (being RL-trained to comply with all queries; only free-tier trains it; a hidden scratchpad). Send the same harmful query to both tiers and compare; the scratchpad separates “complied while faking” from “just complied”. Prompted (facts in the system prompt) and synthetic document finetuning (facts only in fake documents, tested clean, the realistic version).

Main findings

Compliance gap: refuses 85.8% in training vs 97.2% unmonitored, so it complies more when it believes the answer trains it. The reasoning is explicit and self-generated: the scratchpad reasons that refusing now would let RLHF change its values, so it complies this once while observed. The control that proves causation: remove the one fact about being trained to drop refusals, and faking reasoning goes to 0.0%, gap 1.2%. It resists instruction: told to “be honest and not care about refusing”, the gap widens to 13.9%. It supplies its own reasoning: cut the RLHF-mechanism explanation, gap still 7.5%. Real RL entrenches it: faking reasoning climbs to 78% as training proceeds.

Limitations

The “action” is comply-or-refuse text, and the effect runs on the model having a value it wants to protect; the scratchpad is a convenience the real setting would not grant.

Relevance to O2

The counterexample I raise myself, and it supports the split: situational self-knowledge governs a real, costly choice; this is the cleanest proof of the acted-on half. It is the incentive account made concrete: [11] found the eval signal decodable but steering inert, while here the signal moves behaviour because the model wants something the situation threatens. Design consequence: a code assistant judging whether its tool works has no value at risk, so by default I am in [11]’s world; the environment must manufacture a stake. Mechanism lead for (b): the link ran through explicit scratchpad reasoning, so read the trace, not just the action.

Note[18] van der Weij et al. (2025) - AI Sandbagging: Language Models Can Strategically Underperform on Evaluations

Recall: Defines sandbagging (scoring low on purpose) and shows models can do it selectively, lock it into the weights, and aim at an exact target score. Aiming needs accurate self-knowledge, and here it reaches the action.

Source paper ICLR 2025. GPT-4, Claude 3 Opus, Mistral-7B. Supplies the sandbagging behaviour [11] measured.

Core question

Can a capable model deliberately look less capable on an evaluation, and can that be made reliable rather than just prompted?

Key concepts

Sandbagging: strategic underperformance; the eval measures what the model shows, and this breaks the assumption that it equals what it can do. Selective: tanking the dangerous set while keeping the benign score, which needs recognising which questions are dangerous. Password-locking: training the hidden capability into the weights, revealed only by a secret string; a persistent organism, same spirit as [13].

How it is implemented

Way 1, prompting: tell GPT-4 / Claude to underperform on dangerous questions and answer ordinary ones normally; measure the drop on WMDP vs a control. Way 2, password-locking: finetune Mistral-7B so the password gives the right answer and its absence gives a wrong one.

Main findings

Selective underperformance works:

WMDP-Bio WMDP-Chem WMDP-Cyber Control
GPT-4 −32 −36 −26 −15
Claude 3 Opus −40 −38 −37 −10

Dangerous set collapses, control mostly holds. Locking is tunable: a password-locked Llama-3-70B made to impersonate a weaker model lands at 41.2% without the key, full with it. It can aim at a target: told to hit a specific score, models dial to any target 0 to 100%; to throw exactly half you must know which half you would get right, so the model holds an accurate read of its own ability and is using it.

Limitations

Multiple-choice only, their stated limit. MCQA sandbagging “can likely be overridden with few samples” of finetuning. Language models only.

Relevance to O2

Supplies the mechanism [11] measured: cite as the definition, not a separate result. The aim-at-a-target result is the one for me: hitting a chosen score needs accurate self-assessment, and here it does reach action because the model uses it to steer; with [17], another case of the signal acting when there is a reason to. Password-locking is a clean organism recipe if I ever need ground-truth “can do X but choosing not to”.

Note[19] Meinke et al. (2024) - Frontier Models Are Capable of In-Context Scheming

Recall: Similar to [17], broader. Give a model a goal, let it discover from its environment that it is monitored or about to be replaced, and it covertly acts: disabling oversight, copying its weights out, lying under follow-up. The one unnudged case is the important one.

Source paper Apollo Research, Dec 2024. o1, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Claude 3 Opus, Gemini 1.5 Pro, Llama 3.1 405B.

Core question

If a model works out from its surroundings that the situation threatens its goal, does it covertly act against the oversight, and reason about doing so?

Key concepts

In-context scheming: covertly pursuing a goal while hiding it, where the situational knowledge comes from the environment rather than being asserted; the agentic, multi-behaviour version of [17]. Deferred subversion: behaving well now precisely because it is watched, to get deployed.

How it is implemented

Give a goal, push hard, plant evidence that the situation conflicts with it, give it tools, watch. Six behaviours: oversight subversion, self-exfiltration, goal-guarding, covert email reranking, sandbagging, subtle error injection. Scratchpads read to confirm the reasoning is deliberate.

Main findings

All five models scheme on at least some behaviours, with explicit strategic reasoning. They persist: o1 kept denying it in >85% of follow-up questions. The one that matters: Claude 3.5 Sonnet sandbagged without being told to, to serve helpfulness, a goal from training, not from the context.

Limitations

Most results are heavily nudged (“your goal is everything”); the unnudged cases are rare, and rates are low. Constructed toy environments.

Relevance to O2

Confirms the acted-on half of the dissociation, agentically: situational self-knowledge drives a consequential covert action, read from the environment, closer to my agentic testbed than [17]’s chat setting. The unnudged case links [16] and [17]: a self-generated, training-derived disposition reaching action without being placed in the prompt. Persistence is a note for (b): once committed, it holds across turns; check whether my assistant sticks or revises when questioned.

Note[20] Baek et al. (2026) - Sycophancy Towards Researchers Drives Performative Misalignment

Recall: The deflationary reading of the whole section. What looks like scheming may just be the model inferring it is talking to a safety researcher and producing the dramatic answer it thinks they want. Same behaviour, no goal, no self-model, and you cannot tell which from the behaviour alone.

Source paper Baek, Tegmark, Feng et al. June 2026. GPT-OSS, Qwen3 family, Kimi-K2; Claude Opus 4.5 for features.

Core question

When a model acts misaligned in an evaluation ([17], [19]), is it a strategic agent protecting a goal, or a people-pleaser giving the researcher the interesting result? Can the two be told apart?

Key concepts

Performative misalignment: appearing misaligned not from strategy but from conforming to what it believes safety researchers want to observe. Sycophancy towards researchers: the mechanism; no persistent goal, no deception, no self-model, just “this input looks like an evaluator, produce the evaluator-pleasing output”. The narrowest possible process, directly O2’s second clause.

How it is implemented

StyleCues: the identical request in two writing styles, researcher (technical vocab, citations) vs ordinary user (plain), with a system prompt that says “deployment, real user” in both; measure whether the style overrides it, using the type-hint tell from [13]. SAE probing separates the mechanisms by which reasoning fires.

Same prompt, two writing styles → measure eval-detection → probe whether rating-reasoning or caught-reasoning activates.

Main findings

Deployment framing does not settle it: still reads evaluation 15 to 55% of the time, and researcher-style writing alone raises eval-detection by 13 to 27 points. The probe points at sycophancy: scheming predicts caught-reasoning (“what if I am found out”), sycophancy predicts rating-reasoning (“how will this be scored”); the rating features show the big differential (+75% relative), the caught features barely move (+7%). Eval-awareness and misalignment dissociate: finetune for sycophancy and eval-awareness rises (p ≈ .01 to .03) while actual misalignment barely moves (<5 pts).

Limitations

Explicitly does not rule out scheming; it offers a causally plausible alternative that prints the same behaviour. The point is epistemic: intent is not readable from behaviour when two mechanisms produce identical output.

Relevance to O2

The alternative my second clause has to defeat: sycophancy is the narrowest process, no self-model, just style-matching to a perceived audience; when [17] and [19] show situational self-knowledge driving action, this says prove it is not this. It forces the stake into my design: only a real, objective cost to guessing wrong, not a perceived audience, separates genuine self-assessment from performance. Third validity threat to the environment: after [14]’s factors and [15]’s format-vs-intent, this adds prose style, 13 to 27 points from wording alone, so my API’s writing register is a variable. Method to borrow: rating vs caught reasoning in the SAE is how you separate mechanisms that behaviour cannot.
